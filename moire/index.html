Inside a moiré qubit

Two crystals a few atoms thick, stacked with a small twist, make a lattice of identical traps for single electrons; here one such device comes apart from the chip down to the trap, followed by six devices from my own calculations and the numbers that decided them.

Faruk Sami Bilgin · November 2026

A moiré material is two atomically thin crystals laid on top of each other with a small twist. Across the stack, the places where the atoms of the two sheets line up and the places where they fall between each other alternate slowly, and that slow alternation, the moiré pattern, repeats over a distance far larger than the spacing of the atoms (the geometry is in chapter 1 of my notes). To an electron the pattern is a superlattice of identical wells, each several nanometres across. Each well can hold one electron or hole, and a trapped carrier is a candidate qubit: a quantum dot made by the crystal rather than by lithography.

The first part of this page takes a typical gated moiré device apart, from the chip on its carrier to the potential that a single hole feels. The second part builds six specific devices from my research, each with the computed numbers that decided whether it works.

Every device on this page is a model built from the geometries in my manuscripts, not a picture of fabricated hardware. None of them has been made. Where a dimension is not in my sources it is drawn at a generic value and marked illustrative.

    Going back up, the order of the layers is the logic of the device. Everything above and below the bilayer is there to shape the landscape of the last step or to keep it clean: boron nitride because it is flat and nearly free of charged defects, graphite because it screens the charges in the oxide, metal gates because a voltage on them can deepen or tilt the wells beneath. The landscape itself comes from the twist alone. That is the promise of moiré qubits, and, as the six devices below show, also where the difficulties begin.

    01
    Twisted hBN

    Artificial atoms in twisted boron nitride

    Analysed device · computed

    Twist two sheets of hexagonal boron nitride and the moiré makes a triangular array of identical wells, one per cell. A recent proposal (Song, Xu and Chang) treats each well as an artificial atom: an electron trapped at the AA site would have a ladder of levels 77.8 meV apart, a "magic angle" at 1.69°, and linewidths below 10 MHz up to 254 K.

    Everything in that picture follows from one object, the moiré potential, so I computed it directly, with density-functional theory at 21 stacking registries. Toggle the figure between the two. The computed well sits where the proposal puts it, at AA, and has the same symmetry, but its equivalent amplitude is \(V_0 = 40\) meV instead of 160 meV, three to four times shallower, and the functional form the proposal uses is forbidden by the symmetry of the stack. The level spacing falls to 26–40 meV, and there is no magic angle.

    A shallow well decides how long an excited electron stays excited. To fall from the first excited level (E) to the ground level (A₁) it must hand about 32 meV to the lattice. A single phonon with that energy carries more momentum than the smooth trapped wavefunction can easily take up, so it couples only weakly; two phonons sharing the energy, each with less momentum, escape that suppression. With them included, \(T_1 \approx 6\)–11 ps. A logical qubit needs about 30 ns.

    What survives is still useful: the wells exist, they are identical, and the A₁→E transition carries a giant dipole, so the array remains interesting as an infrared emitter. As an orbital qubit it does not work.

    02
    Twisted MoS₂

    A hole spin in twisted MoS₂

    Proposed device · computed

    Twisted bilayer MoS₂ is dark. Its valence band peaks at Γ and its conduction band at K, so for the lowest excitons to emit light the moiré would have to supply about 16 of its reciprocal vectors at 2°; it supplies about one. It cannot be an optical qubit. But the same band structure puts the top of the valence band where a hole has no valley and no spin–orbit coupling: a plain spin ½ in every moiré well, a natural quantum dot whose envelope, about 1.2 nm in radius, covers about 320 nuclei.

    Those nuclei are the problem. A quarter of natural molybdenum atoms carry a nuclear spin (⁹⁵Mo and ⁹⁷Mo), and their random sum acts on the hole as a magnetic field it cannot tell from a real one: \(T_2^* \approx 42\) ns. Isotope purification, ⁹⁸Mo and ³²S, which have no nuclear spin, removes them. Move the slider: \(T_2^*\) grows as one over the square root of the fraction that remains.

    It grows until the encapsulation takes over. Boron and nitrogen have no spin-free isotope, so the hBN nuclei just outside the bilayer set a floor: 0.79 µs with the field perpendicular to the plane, 1.3 µs with it in plane. Independent noise sources add as

    $$\frac{1}{T_2^{*2}} = \frac{1}{T_{2,\mathrm{MoS_2}}^{*2}} + \frac{1}{T_{2,\mathrm{hBN}}^{*2}},$$

    so a highly enriched, hBN-encapsulated device reaches 0.7–1.0 µs, the level of natural silicon. Going further would need a spinless spacer between the bilayer and the hBN.

    03
    Twisted WSe₂

    Valley qubits: by wire or by light

    Analysed devices · computed

    In twisted WSe₂ the qubit can live in the valley of a hole, which of two corners of the Brillouin zone it occupies. There are two ways to control it. A gate-defined dot, proposed by Saleem and co-workers, confines a hole under electrodes; the dot's lowest level splits into a valley doublet, and gate voltages drive it. The alternative uses light: at small twist one hole sits in each moiré cell (a Mott insulator), and two laser beams rotate its spin–valley state through a trapped trion, a Raman gate.

    I compared both in the same material on one pipeline, checked against the magic angle (1.42° predicted, 1.43° published) and against the proposal's own ladder of valley splittings. The comparison is not symmetric in angle: each paradigm works at a different twist of the same crystal. The gate dot sits at 5.08°; the Mott insulator needs small angles, where the ratio of interaction to bandwidth reaches 10²–10³, and at 5.08° that ratio has collapsed to 2.7. Toggle the figure between the two.

    Electrically, the doublet splitting depends steeply on the size of the dot: 382, 55.3 and 3.41 µeV for radii of 5, 7 and 10 moiré periods. Optically, a π gate takes 0.22 ns at an error of 4 × 10⁻³, but only if the branching ratio β, the fraction of trion decays that return to the other qubit state, is near one; spin–valley locking keeps it small, and no knob is known that makes it large. The lasers need 0.02–11 mW per site.

    Twist disorder hits both. At a specification of σθ = 0.036°, the electrical dots spread by 4.5 % and need per-site calibration; the optical sites spread over about 17 frequency channels. At the disorder actually measured in twisted WSe₂, more than 1° over micrometres, both budgets fail.

    04
    MoSe₂ / WSe₂

    An exciton emitter in a nanobeam cavity

    Analysed device · computed

    In a heterobilayer of two different crystals, MoSe₂ on WSe₂, an electron in one layer and a hole in the other bind into an interlayer exciton. The moiré traps it, and each trapped exciton is a quantum emitter. On its own it is slow: 139 ns in my calibrated pipeline, against about 200 ns measured. A cavity can speed it up, the Purcell effect, by giving the photon a resonant mode to go into.

    In the textbook, the enhancement grows with the cavity's quality factor \(Q\). A real emitter, though, has a linewidth \(\gamma\), and once the cavity line \(E/Q\) is narrower than the emitter's, more \(Q\) buys nothing. The dephasing-corrected budget is

    $$F_\mathrm{eff} = \frac{3}{4\pi^2}\,\frac{\xi}{V}\,\frac{E}{E/Q + \gamma},$$

    which saturates at \(Q_{1/2} = E/\gamma \approx 1.4\times10^4\) for the best linewidth reported, 100 µeV, and at \(2.1\times10^3\) for the 640 µeV line measured in a cavity at 10 K. Slide \(Q\) and watch the curve flatten.

    So the currency is not \(Q\) but the mode volume \(V\) and the overlap \(\xi\), the share of the peak field the emitter actually sits in. My 3D FDTD computation of a suspended silicon-nitride nanobeam gives \(Q = 7.9\times10^3\) and \(V = 0.19\,(\lambda/n)^3\), but a crystal laid on top sees only \(\xi = 0.032\): a tenfold penalty for sitting on the surface, which the small volume only partly repays.

    05
    Magic-angle graphene

    A twisted-graphene junction in a sub-terahertz cavity

    Proposed device · computed

    Twisted bilayer graphene at its magic angle, 1.025° in my model, where the flat bands are 2.9 meV wide, superconducts. Gate a strip of it between two superconducting leads and you have a Josephson junction whose current–phase relation is skewed by its high transparency, and a skewed relation shows half-integer Shapiro steps under microwaves (chapter 3 of my notes; the junction itself is explained in the long read).

    The design puts the junction in the defect of a silicon photonic-crystal slab: three missing holes, an L3 cavity, in a lattice of 587 µm, which traps a mode at 145 GHz with an in-situ \(Q_i = 1.46\times10^4\). The cavity is millimetres across, the junction micrometres; the slider zooms from one to the other. A bare van der Waals stack would barely feel the cavity, about 1 µV of drive instead of 600 µV, so an engineered collection structure 20–25 µm long is part of the design.

    At \(I_c = 300\) nA and \(R_n = 200\) Ω, the half step is \(0.200\,I_c\), 60 nA, wide: four times the floor set by a sinusoidal junction. It survives heating only while \(E_J/k_BT > 55.5\), so the electrons must be colder than 129 mK; a parametric estimate puts them near 0.6 K at the reference drive.

    A companion study draws the limits. The step measures transparency, not mechanism: trapped vortices and multiple Andreev reflection mimic its dependence on filling and twist. A quantum-geometric readout is ruled out, the Higgs mode is overdamped (\(Q_H \approx 1.5\)), and 145 GHz sits at or above the pair-breaking edge, \(\hbar\omega/2\Delta = 0.99\)–1.9. A safe, adiabatic drive is 15–29 GHz.

    06
    Bilayer graphene

    Shaking a quantum dot does not quiet it

    Proposed device · computed · refuted

    In bilayer graphene a perpendicular electric field opens a band gap, and gates can carve a quantum dot out of the gapped sheet. Its weakness is charge noise: any fluctuating charge nearby shifts the dot's levels. I asked whether shaking the confinement, modulating the dot potential at a frequency well above the qubit's, could average that noise away and make a dynamical sweet spot.

    The model is a four-band continuum with a Gaussian dot 15 nm wide and 20 meV deep at a displacement gap of 0.167 eV, where the two lowest levels are 0.989 meV apart; an independent atomistic solver reproduces that to three digits. Averaged over one period of the drive, the modulation seemed to help: T₂* rose by 9 % at an amplitude of 50 meV.

    It does not survive. The averaging argument needs the level splitting to bend down as a function of the field; it bends up (curvature +0.48). And when the drive is followed in time, in the full space of states, it throws the electron out of the two qubit levels: 38–95 % of the population leaves every period at the amplitudes where the protection appeared. Where the qubit survives, the effect is negligible. The slider steps through the computed amplitudes.

    What did survive is symmetry: a mirror-symmetric double dot, and a spin qubit inside one valley, are protected; the tunable sweet spots were not.

    07
    Scale

    Why none of these is a logical qubit yet

    A logical qubit is not a better physical qubit. It is hundreds of physical qubits, each controlled individually, running error correction together. I asked whether a moiré platform could reach 300 logical qubits, starting from its best material, isotopically purified twisted MoS₂ (device 2), and attacking every design with an independent reviewer who wrote separate code. The material and the qubit type survived. The control layer did not.

    The reason is a mismatch of scales. To address one bond of the array, a control line must set an energy to within 5 µeV to 0.23 meV, depending on the scheme; the moiré sites themselves scatter by about 15 meV. A metal line 3–20 nm above the plane spreads its potential over 16–44 nm, wider than the 8 nm bond it is meant to address. The least-bad scheme needs 607–809 thousand analog lines for 300 logical qubits; an active matrix would cut the wiring, but it needs a memory node at a 12.5–14 nm pitch that no technology offers. Until site disorder falls to 3–5 meV and such a node exists, moiré qubits remain a research platform, and the main line for a large processor is a conventional gate-defined one.
