Taking a quantum computer apart

A superconducting quantum computer is mostly refrigerator. The qubits are patches of aluminium on a chip a few millimetres across; keeping them quantum takes a machine taller than a person, colder than outer space, and wired with dozens of cables that must not let any heat in. Here it comes apart one layer at a time, from the closed cryostat down to the few atoms that make a qubit a qubit.

Faruk Sami Bilgin · November 2026

Each step removes one part and says what it was for. Notice the pattern as you go down: every layer exists to keep heat, noise or stray radiation away from the layer inside it, and every stage is colder and smaller than the one around it, until the last thing left is a barrier of oxide a few atoms thick.

    Going back up, the order is the logic of the whole machine. The barrier is the qubit's only nonlinear element, and it works only while nothing breaks the Cooper pairs that tunnel through it and nothing excites the circuit around it. So the film is aluminium, superconducting below about 1.2 K; the chip sits in a closed copper box inside a magnetic shield; the lines that reach it are attenuated and filtered stage by stage; the amplifiers that listen to it are cold and close; and the whole stack is wrapped in shields at successively lower temperatures, inside a vacuum. Everything above the junction is there so that the junction sees almost nothing but the pulses we send it.
